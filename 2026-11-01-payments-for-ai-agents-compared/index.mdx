An agent needs one piece of data before it can finish a task. The provider charges a fraction of a cent. The agent has a budget, the data is available, and both sides would benefit from the exchange. Then comes the payment integration.

Can the agent pay that small amount directly? Does it need another currency for fees? When can the provider return the data? What happens if the request times out after money has moved?

Atto fits AI-agent micropayments that need separate transfers without protocol fees and wallet-enforced spending limits, provided you can build the service's payment logic. USDC on Base or Solana may fit dollar budgets and existing x402 services better; Lightning fits bitcoin services using L402. Nano also offers feeless transfers and community agent integrations.

We build Atto for small, direct payments. This comparison follows each option from a funded wallet to a delivered API response, including costs, confirmation and integration readiness.

## Compare the payment you need to make

The comparison below focuses on **$0.001 API calls paid separately**. The USDC economics scores use Coinbase's CDP Facilitator with `exact`, after its monthly free allowance. Other providers and batching can change those scores. Atto and Nano are assessed on direct protocol transfers; Lightning depends on the route and channel setup. These are payment-charge comparisons, not total operating-cost estimates.

For **payment acceptance**, Atto and Nano need a confirmed Send with the correct recipient and amount. The service can deliver the purchase then; a later Receive makes the merchant's funds spendable. The observed averages below have their own dates and measurement boundaries. They are useful network observations, not a matched client-to-API benchmark. Integration complexity concerns building the complete paid service, not just installing a wallet.

## When a $1 workload leaves nothing to run the service

**1,000 calls at $0.001 each bring in $1.** Suppose each call needs one successful CDP `exact` settlement and the monthly free allowance has already been used. At **$0.001 per onchain transaction**, those calls incur **$1 in facilitator fees**, leaving **$0 before serving the requests**. This is published-tariff arithmetic, not a live transaction benchmark. The first 1,000 monthly transactions are free; verification is also free. [CDP pricing](https://docs.cdp.coinbase.com/x402/seller/facilitator)

The fee already covers settlement gas. It should not be added to a second Base gas bill. CDP's [FAQ](https://docs.cdp.coinbase.com/x402/support/faq) explicitly identifies the zero-margin problem at this price. It applies to individual settlements on both Base and Solana through that service.

Under that tariff, separately settled calls priced at or below $0.001 leave no revenue to cover service costs once the allowance is exhausted. Below $0.001, the processing fee alone exceeds revenue. Larger small payments—what we mean here by **mini payments**—have more room: the same fee takes 10% of a $0.01 sale or 1% of a $0.10 sale, before other costs.

Atto and Nano deduct no protocol transfer fee from 1,000 direct transfers. Funding, conversion, computational work and infrastructure still cost resources. Batching or a different facilitator can also improve USDC economics, but changes the payment setup being compared.

USDC aims to track the US dollar, making dollar-denominated budgets easier to manage than balances in a floating-price asset. That introduces issuer and redemption dependencies. Circle can block addresses from sending or receiving USDC; its [USDC terms](https://www.circle.com/legal/usdc-terms) describe the restrictions and distinguish EEA holders. A stablecoin does not eliminate pricing, custody or access risk.

### Atto: direct tiny payments with bounded wallet access

Atto supports nine decimal places and charges no protocol transaction fee. Direct transfers need neither a separate gas token nor payment-channel funding. That makes it worth evaluating for individual API calls, data lookups and tool actions where the service accepts ATTO. The [technical whitepaper](/docs/whitepaper/technical) explains its computational work, voting and infrastructure costs.

[Atto MCP](/build/mcp) gives an assistant wallet operations, starts read-only, and requires local approval to enable spending with [per-payment and rolling spending caps](/docs/integration/mcp#approve-a-spending-allowance). Its allowance controls constrain payments through that wallet profile; they do not constrain arbitrary programs with unrestricted access to the same computer. The [CLI](/build/cli), [N8N integration](/build/n8n) and [Commons libraries](/build/commons) serve other application shapes.

The boundary matters. The CLI and MCP packages are in beta. A wallet tool that sends ATTO does not automatically negotiate an API price, match payment to an order, or deliver the purchased response. Our [micropayment-rails roadmap](/docs/roadmap#phase-7-micropayment-rails) still lists the x402-style flow and paid demonstration endpoint as planned work.

Atto fits developers who want small direct transfers, can work with ATTO-denominated balances, and can implement the service's payment logic. If a working paid-HTTP integration is the immediate requirement, compare that remaining work with the published x402 and L402 tooling below. ATTO's exchange rate can move, and funding or converting it can have separate costs.

### Nano: feeless transfers with community agent integrations

Nano also offers transfers without a protocol fee. Its [protocol overview](https://docs.nano.org/what-is-nano/overview/) explains account chains, representative voting and computational work. A confirmed send creates a receivable; a receive makes it spendable.

Alongside [RPC and wallet interfaces](https://docs.nano.org/integration-guides/), [Nano's agent page](https://nano.org/agents) links community x402 client/server tooling, and [Nano Hub](https://hub.nano.org/developer-tools/ai/299) lists MCP integrations. These can supply agent and paid-HTTP interfaces; check the supported client, server, network and versions.

Nano's agent page notes that the linked community implementation returns a block hash without explicitly waiting for confirmation. Its [security notes](https://github.com/x402nano/exact#security-notes-) warn of limited production deployment and recommend testing with small amounts. Check confirmation and failed-delivery recovery. For custom wallet integrations, the internal node wallet is unsupported for production; use third-party or custom key management.

Nano fits services accepting XNO and developers evaluating its community agent tools. XNO has a floating exchange rate. Work, hosting and key-management costs remain, whether operated internally or provided by someone else.

### USDC on Base: dollar pricing with an HTTP payment path

USDC on Base is a useful candidate when the service prices in dollars and supports the corresponding x402 payment option. The agent can authorize a payment as part of an HTTP exchange instead of requiring a separate checkout at each step. The [x402 buyer quickstart](https://docs.x402.org/getting-started/quickstart-for-buyers) shows the client integration path.

Direct Base transfers carry [execution and Ethereum publication fees](https://docs.base.org/specifications/transactions/network-fees). Estimate the fee for the actual signed transfer and current network conditions. The hosted CDP route instead covers gas within its own tariff; sponsorship changes the payer, not the underlying economics.

Base distinguishes [sequencer preconfirmation, L2 inclusion and later Ethereum finality](https://docs.base.org/specifications/transactions/transaction-finality). A service returning a response after an early acknowledgement accepts a different risk than one waiting for Ethereum finality. Those stages do not provide a measured submission-to-acceptance time for this USDC flow; queueing and inclusion delays also matter.

This option fits dollar-denominated services using existing x402 tooling, with prices large enough for their payment costs. Under CDP's paid per-settlement tariff, a $0.001 purchase cannot fund the service delivering it. That is an economic limit of this setup, rather than a protocol rule forbidding tiny USDC amounts.

### USDC on Solana: dollar payments with a different operating model

USDC on Solana addresses the same denomination requirement through another network. x402's [network and token support](https://docs.x402.org/core-concepts/network-and-token-support) describes supported configurations; verify that the buyer, seller and facilitator support the same combination. An agent already using Solana may benefit from keeping its payments within that environment.

Solana charges transaction fees in SOL. Its [payment documentation](https://solana.com/docs/payments/how-payments-work) explains that a separate fee payer can cover them, so the agent need not always manage SOL itself. Sending a token to a new recipient can also involve creating a token account. That requires a [refundable storage deposit](https://solana.com/docs/tokens/basics/create-token-account), separate from the transaction fee. Existing recipient accounts do not need that setup for every payment.

[On closure](https://solana.com/docs/tokens/basics/close-account), the account owner or close authority chooses the refund destination; the original payer does not automatically recover it.

For speed, time the actual USDC payment from client submission through the chosen confirmation assurance and recipient balance update. Include RPC submission, any queueing and retries. A consensus target or a fast RPC response does not measure that payment path.

This option fits Solana USDC services and compatible tooling. Its [native transaction fee](https://solana.com/docs/core/fees) starts at 5,000 lamports per signature, plus any priority fee. CDP's hosted tariff remains a separate operating choice: fast consensus does not remove a facilitator's per-settlement charge. Recipient setup and issuer dependencies remain relevant.

### Bitcoin Lightning: repeated payments through funded channels

Lightning moves bitcoin through payment channels rather than recording every small payment as a separate Bitcoin base-layer transaction. For services already accepting bitcoin, that can support repeated small payments with an existing API access mechanism.

[L402](https://docs.lightning.engineering/the-lightning-network/l402/l402) combines a Lightning invoice with an access token. Paying the invoice reveals a proof that the client can present with the token. [Aperture](https://docs.lightning.engineering/lightning-network-tools/aperture) provides a reverse-proxy implementation for putting that mechanism in front of services.

Its operating requirements deserve equal attention. Sending requires outbound capacity; receiving requires inbound capacity. Lightning Labs' [liquidity guide](https://docs.lightning.engineering/the-lightning-network/liquidity/how-to-get-inbound-capacity-on-the-lightning-network) explains the funding and routing considerations. Channel operations can incur Bitcoin network fees, routed payments can incur routing fees, and a provider may add its own charges. A usable route and sufficient liquidity remain necessary.

Lightning fits bitcoin-denominated services and developers willing to operate that infrastructure or choose a provider. Self-managed channels bring liquidity and availability work. A custodial service can simplify the experience while adding reliance on that custodian. Bitcoin's dollar value also moves; using sats as the payment unit does not create a dollar peg.

## Wallet access and paid API access solve different steps

MCP connects an assistant to tools. A payment network transfers value. HTTP payment protocols describe how a service requests payment and how a client proves authorization or payment. An application may use all three.

For example, [x402](https://docs.x402.org/core-concepts/http-402) uses an HTTP 402 response to communicate payment requirements. The client retries with a payment payload; the integration verifies authorization and handles settlement according to the scheme. L402 uses a Lightning payment proof to authorize access.

Batch settlement can move authorized payments onchain later and spread costs across calls. It also introduces channel funding and settlement lifecycle work. CDP currently supports it on EVM, but its `createX402Server` helper does not register that scheme; it needs a separate supported integration. [Current scheme support](https://docs.cdp.coinbase.com/x402/support/faq)

For every option, establish what the service accepts, what the agent can sign, who covers costs, and which event permits delivery. Enforce spending limits in the wallet or payment service, outside the agent's prompt. Keep the purchase reference, payment identifier and delivered result connected. After a timeout, check whether payment succeeded before creating another one.

## Confirmation is enough to serve the purchase

For Atto and Nano, a verified confirmed Send is enough for payment acceptance and delivery. Check its destination and amount, and match it to the purchase. The recipient can publish Receive later to make those funds spendable; the buyer should not wait for that operation to get the resource.

For an end-to-end confirmation test, start the clock when the client submits the payment and stop when the service can verify payment acceptance. ## Which payment option fits your AI agent?

If the service already accepts one of these options, begin there. Supporting a network that the seller cannot receive does not complete the purchase.

For dollar-denominated budgets and an available x402 integration, investigate USDC on Base or Solana, including the actual facilitator terms and completion policy. For bitcoin services with an L402 interface, investigate Lightning and how liquidity and custody are handled.

Evaluate Atto when you want separate payments without protocol fees, locally approved wallet spending limits, and control over the paid-service implementation. Evaluate Nano for feeless transfers with community x402 or MCP tooling. For either, test the complete payment, confirmation and delivery path.

You may also find that a prepaid service balance is simpler for a particular workload. Funding once and metering calls internally can reduce repeated payment work, while introducing a balance held with that provider.

To evaluate Atto, start with the [integration guides](/docs/integration), fund a small wallet through the [faucet](/faucet), and test the payment and recovery path. The useful result is a payment flow that fits the task, respects the agent's budget and delivers the response the user needed.
